## The setup

| | Training | Scoring |
|---|---|---|
| Observation cutoff | 2025-06-01 | 2025-12-01 |
| Customers | 4,922 | 4,376 |
| Churned in the next 90 days | 12.7% | 11.8% |
| Excluded (churned in the earlier window) | — | 624 |

Features are built strictly from facts dated on or before each cutoff. 3,937 customers are used to fit the model and 985 are held back for calibration, so the calibrator never sees the data its calibration is reported on.

The scoring population excludes customers who churned during the earlier window: they are gone, and scoring a model on customers who already left measures nothing. That exclusion is also why the two base rates differ — the surviving base is lower-risk than the one the model was trained on.

## Three readings of the same model

The same features and the same estimator, evaluated three ways. Only the first is honest.

| Reading | AUC | What it actually measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Out of time** (fit at 2025-06-01, scored at 2025-12-01) | **0.690** | Whether the model generalises *forward*, which is the only thing production asks of it |
| In-time random split | 0.709 | Whether it generalises to customers it did not see, in the period it was fitted on |
| With one post-outcome feature | 1.000 | Nothing. The feature is derived from the label |

A random split reads **+0.020 AUC** against the out-of-time result — and that is where the reporting has to stay honest. The out-of-time AUC carries a standard error of ±0.014 (Hanley–McNeil), so a gap of 0.020 is inside two standard errors. **This dataset does not establish that the in-time reading is optimistic.** It usually is, and the generating process here is stationary by construction so there is little drift for it to miss — but the honest statement is that the difference is within noise.

The reason to evaluate out of time is therefore **methodological, not empirical**. A random split answers a question production never asks — *how does this do on customers I have not seen, during the period I fitted on?* — and it cannot detect drift, seasonality, or a population that has changed shape, because it holds none of them out. On a real base those effects are the difference between a model that works and one that quietly stopped working. The number to publish is the first row, and it would still be the first row if the gap were zero.

The third row is the one worth sitting with: adding a single field derived from the outcome takes AUC to 1.000. Nothing errors, no test fails, and the model is worthless. Leakage does not announce itself — it shows up as a result good enough that nobody questions it.

## Does it rank, and does it mean anything?

Two different questions. A model can pass the first and fail the second.

| Metric | Value | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| AUC | 0.690 ± 0.014 | Ranking. Typical for churn on behavioural features |
| KS | 0.291 | Separation between churners and stayers |
| Top-decile lift | 2.68× | The riskiest 10% churn 2.68× the base rate |
| Top-decile capture | 26.8% | Share of all churn sitting in that 10% |
| Brier score | 0.0979 | Probability accuracy (lower is better) |
| Log loss | 0.3380 | Penalises confident mistakes |
| Expected calibration error | 0.0193 | Mean gap between promised and observed risk |
| Calibration slope | 0.77 | 1.00 = confidence exactly right; below 1 = overconfident |

Accuracy is deliberately absent. With a base rate of 11.8%, predicting "nobody churns" scores 88.2% accuracy and is worth nothing.

![Calibration](calibration.svg)

**Calibration is where the time gap shows.** The slope is 0.77, not 1.00: the model is overconfident six months on. It predicts 12.2% churn on average against 11.8% observed. Recalibrating on the training period does not fix this (ECE 0.0134 raw vs 0.0193 after Platt scaling) — the base rate moved *after* the calibrator was fitted, and no adjustment made on old data anticipates that. The calibrated model is what every figure above and below uses; that the raw one scores marginally better here is the finding, not a reason to drop the step. Holding data out to calibrate remains correct method; what it cannot do is see forward.

With hindsight the ceiling is ECE 0.0077 and slope 1.00 — recalibrating on the outcomes we were trying to predict. That is not achievable in production; it is reported to separate the two failure modes. The ranking is fine. Only the level has drifted. So the operational answer is not a better model: it is to recalibrate against the most recent closed window and to alarm on predicted-vs-actual base rate as a drift monitor.

## What drives it

Coefficients on standardised features, so they are comparable across features. Two columns, because they answer different questions: **marginal** is the effect on its own — what a stakeholder means by "do unhappy customers churn more?" — and **conditional** is the effect given every other feature, which is what the model uses. Quoting the second as if it were the first is how an explainable model gets explained wrong.

Because this dataset's causal structure is *known* (`data-model/telco/config.py`), the last column is a real check rather than a matter of taste. Of the 9 designed drivers that survive pruning: **5 recovered** with the correct sign, **0 contradicted**, and **4 are too weak to read individually** — their marginal effect is smaller than its own standard error. That last group is not a failure: some drivers are deliberately weak by design (a payment-problem *rate* around 0.1 against a weight of 1.1 moves the log-odds by about 0.11), and reporting the sign of a coefficient smaller than its noise would be the analytical error, not the finding.

*⇄ marks a feature whose sign changes once the other features are present — not a bug, and not leakage: it means another feature already carries its information.*

Three features were dropped before the coefficients were read, because each duplicated one already kept:

- `has_unresolved_escalation` — correlated +0.951 with `unresolved_escalations`
- `monthly_fee` — correlated +0.997 with `arpu_last3`
- `plan_tier` — correlated +0.929 with `arpu_last3`

This is not about accuracy; the ridge penalty absorbs collinearity and AUC barely moves. It is about the deliverable. Fitted unpruned, this model reports that *an unresolved escalation reduces churn* — the exact opposite of how the data was built. A model whose explanation is wrong is worse than one with no explanation, because someone will act on it.

**The most interesting row is `retention_offer_taken`.** On its own it looks inert (-0.045, inside its own noise). Conditional on risk it is one of the strongest protective effects in the model (-0.290). That is confounding, and it is in the data on purpose: retention campaigns were *targeted at high-risk customers*, so the raw comparison hides a real effect. Untangling it properly needs the held-out control group — which is case 05, not this one. Here it is a warning: this coefficient is not a campaign ROI, and reporting it as one would be wrong.

## The decision: who do we actually call?

A churn model retains nobody. A retention action does, and there is budget for a fraction of the base. So the deliverable is a contact list, and the ranking that builds it is a commercial choice, not a statistical one.

| Assumption | Value |
|---|---|
| Cost per contact | 1.50 |
| Cost of the offer, when accepted | 12.00 |
| Share of would-be churners a contact retains | 25.0% |
| Contribution margin | 35.0% |
| Value horizon | 12 months |

These are the retention team's numbers, not measurements. Every figure below inherits them.

![Profit by capacity](profit.svg)

At a budget of 437 contacts (10.0% of the base):

| Targeting | Realised profit |
|---|---:|
| Ranked by predicted risk | 2,772 |
| Ranked by expected value | 4,595 |
| **Difference** | **+1,824** |

Same model, same probabilities, same budget — **+1,824** from changing only the sort order. The two lists share just 43.9% of their names: ranking by risk promotes customers who are likely to leave but cheap to lose, and a contact spent there is a contact not spent on someone worth keeping.

The curves also peak in different places. Risk-ranked peaks at 3,282 contacts (7,290); value-ranked peaks at 2,188 (8,266) — **more profit from fewer calls.** Contacting the entire base returns 6,724, which is what happens when the model is used to justify a campaign rather than to size one.

## Limitations

- **The save rate is assumed, not measured.** Realised profit applies a flat 25.0% to customers who did churn. Whether a contact *caused* a save is unknowable without a control group — that is case 05, and until it exists these figures are a planning model, not a measured return.
- **One historical split.** Two cutoffs give one out-of-time estimate, not a distribution. A rolling backtest over several cutoffs would put an error bar on the AUC gap; this reports a point.
- **Synthetic data, with a known generating process.** That is what makes the driver check possible, and it also means the model is not being tested against the messiness that breaks real pipelines: missing history, merged accounts, plan migrations, delayed billing.
- **Consent is static** in the data model, so it cannot be re-derived as of a past cutoff. It is not used here; case 03 has to confront it.
- **A 90-day window is a choice.** Shorter windows raise precision and shrink the base of actionable churn; longer ones do the reverse. Nothing here establishes that 90 days is the right horizon for a given retention operation.

## What would make this good enough to deploy

Stated as tests, not aspirations:

- Top-decile capture stays above 26.8% on the next unseen cutoff.
- Calibration slope stays within 0.85–1.15, checked every cycle, with recalibration triggered on breach rather than on a schedule.
- Predicted vs actual base rate is monitored as a drift alarm — it moved here, and it will move again.
- The contact list is generated by expected value, not probability, and the economics behind it are reviewed with the retention team rather than assumed by the modeller.
- A held-out control on every retention campaign, so the save rate stops being an assumption.
